\begin{abstract}
In countries without a centralised national suicide registry, newspaper reports are often the only public source of case-level data, yet they are severely incomplete, with raw missingness reaching up to 90\% on the sparsest fields. We assemble 1{,}617 newspaper-reported suicide cases from Bangladesh (2017--2025), extending a public collection with newly collected cases, and study whether principled imputation can render such data usable for unsupervised analysis. We propose a pipeline that first consolidates the raw free-text categorical labels into a compact vocabulary (918 unique values reduced to 156 canonical categories), and then benchmarks three imputation methods (MissForest, MICE, and $k$-nearest neighbours) against two clustering algorithms (K-Means and Agglomerative) under a six-component, balance-aware composite cluster-validity score. We compare each imputed cell against no-imputation baselines that simply drop the incomplete columns and rows, giving ten pipelines in total (six imputed and four baseline). Rather than commit to one weighting of the six metrics, we test robustness directly. Across 100{,}000 weight vectors sampled uniformly over the metric simplex, the four strong imputed pipelines occupy the top four ranks in over 99.9\% of weightings, while the two poorly-combined imputed pipelines and all four baselines are essentially never among the best. Imputation therefore helps irrespective of how the metrics are weighted. The gain is nonetheless conditional on the pairing of imputer and clustering algorithm, with two imputed pipelines falling below the best baseline. We report no single best pipeline (the top rank alternates between two imputed pipelines), yet the strong pipelines converge on the same clean, cohort-aligned two-cluster structure. The results indicate that careful imputation methodology is a feasible foundation for suicide surveillance in low-resource, news-derived public-health data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:Introduction}

Suicide is a leading cause of death among young people and an explicit concern of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. Target 3.4 of Goal 3 (SDG-3) calls for a one-third reduction in premature mortality from non-communicable diseases and mental-health conditions by 2030, and the suicide mortality rate is tracked as indicator~3.4.2~\cite{un2015sdg,who2024sdg34}. Effective prevention depends on case-level surveillance data that many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) do not systematically collect. National suicide registries are absent or fragmentary, and where suicide is criminalised the official records are further suppressed~\cite{arafat2025surveillance}. In Bangladesh, as in many LMICs, newspaper reports are among the only public sources of case-level information on suicide, and they have been used to study its demography and risk factors~\cite{himel2024studentnews}.

News-derived data, however, are ill-suited to standard machine-learning workflows. Reporting is incidental rather than systematic, so many key fields (occupation, location, financial circumstances, prior mental-health history) are missing for roughly half to over 90\% of cases in the raw reports, and the missingness is structured rather than random. Listwise deletion is not viable. In our corpus only $3.5\%$ of records are complete across all retained fields, so dropping incomplete rows would discard the overwhelming majority of the data, while restricting to the few well-populated columns throws away most of the feature space. The practical question for surveillance in this regime is therefore not which model to fit, but whether the data can be made usable at all.

We address this with a transparent pipeline on a dataset of 1{,}617 newspaper-reported suicide cases from Bangladesh (2017 to 2025). The pipeline first consolidates the raw free-text categorical labels into a compact canonical vocabulary. It then benchmarks three imputation methods against two clustering algorithms under a six-component composite score that rewards well-balanced clusters. We also compare each imputed pipeline against a no-imputation baseline that simply drops the incomplete columns and rows. The study asks whether such a pipeline can surface stable and interpretable structure in severely incomplete newspaper data, and how this compares with dropping the incomplete records instead.

This study pursues three objectives.
\begin{itemize}
\item To construct a comprehensive dataset of newspaper-reported suicide cases in Bangladesh by extending an existing public dataset with newly collected records.
\item To systematically evaluate the effectiveness of different imputation and clustering method combinations against no-imputation baselines using a balance-aware cluster-validity metric.
\item To examine the robustness of imputation-based clustering performance across diverse metric weightings and identify the most effective imputation--clustering combinations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related-works}

Newspaper reports are widely used to monitor suicide in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where official data are limited. Arafat~\cite{arafat2025surveillance} argues that many LMICs lack a centralised suicide registry and that, where suicide is criminalised, official records are further suppressed, leaving newspaper reports as a primary public source of case-level information. The work motivates the data-scarcity premise of this paper but proposes surveillance reform rather than a computational pipeline for the resulting data.

Himel et al.~\cite{himel2024studentnews} analyse post-pandemic student suicides drawn from Bangladeshi online-news portals, the same medium and country as ours. Their study is descriptive and manual, like other news-based content analyses. It shows that newspapers are a viable substrate, but it leaves the severe, structured missingness of such data untreated. That gap is what our work addresses.

The three estimators we benchmark, MissForest, MICE, and $k$-nearest-neighbour imputation, are the field's de facto baselines. We detail and cite them in Section~\ref{sec:Methodology}.

J\"ager et al.~\cite{jager2021benchmark} present a broad benchmark of imputation methods on tabular data, finding that iterative, model-based methods generally lead on mixed-type data. The comparison is rigorous but conducted on complete data with artificially induced missingness.

Shadbahr et al.~\cite{shadbahr2023imputationquality} show that imputation quality has measurable downstream effects on classifier performance. This finding directly motivates our downstream-validity evaluation. However, they too rely on withheld ground truth.

Prakash et al.~\cite{prakash2025mentalhealthimpute} benchmark imputation in large-scale mental-health survey databases, the closest domain to ours. The data, however, are survey records with artificially induced missingness rather than organically incomplete news reports. Closest in spirit to our approach, Aracri et al.~\cite{aracri2025imputation} compare KNN, MICE, and MissForest on dementia data and, like us, judge them by their effect on a downstream task rather than by per-cell reconstruction.

Unsupervised clustering has been widely used to identify subgroups in suicide and mental-health data. Sanz-Gomez et al.~\cite{sanzgomez2025impulsivity} applied k-means clustering to profile suicide deaths, Namgung et al.~\cite{namgung2024subgroups} used clustering to separate psychiatric outpatients into distinct suicide-risk subgroups, and Wong et al.~\cite{wong2022studentclusters} classified student suicides into typologies. These studies operate on largely complete clinical or registry records rather than the severely incomplete, news-derived data treated here. Because such analyses carry no ground-truth labels, internal cluster-validity indices substitute for them. Rousseeuw's silhouette coefficient~\cite{rousseeuw1987silhouette} is the most widely used. Chicco et al.~\cite{chicco2025silhouette} show that no single index suffices and that the silhouette and Davies--Bouldin indices are often the most informative, which is why we adopt a multi-index composite.

Each prior thread thus covers only part of this space. Newspaper-based suicide studies perform neither imputation nor a clustering benchmark. Clustering analyses of suicide operate on largely complete registry data. And imputation benchmarks target complete survey or tabular data with artificially induced missingness. To our knowledge, this paper is among the first to combine news-derived suicide data with a multi-method imputation benchmark, multi-algorithm clustering, released code, and explicit source-cohort confound analysis.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:Methodology}


This section describes the dataset and how we prepared it. It then details the modelling pipeline that produces and scores the clustering results. A final subsection summarises the whole procedure as an algorithm.

\subsection{Data Description and Preprocessing}
The dataset contains 1{,}617 newspaper-reported suicide cases from Bangladesh. We built it by extending a public 2020 collection of 760 cases~\cite{alom2020bangladesh} with 857 cases collected manually from verified national outlets (Prothom Alo, The Daily Star, Jugantor, Ittefaq) covering 2017 to 2019 and 2021 to 2025. Five fields with more than 70\% missing values were removed, because imputing such sparse fields adds more uncertainty than signal. This left 29 analytical features, 13 categorical and 16 numerical.

Missingness is severe and structured, and it is the dominant modelling obstacle. Per-column missing rates range from under 3\% on the best-reported fields, such as method, reason, and age, to over 90\% on the sparsest (Fig.~\ref{fig:missingness}). Five fields sat above the 70\% threshold and were dropped, including prior suicide attempt at 91.7\% and duration of separation at 96.2\%. The gaps are not random. Sensitive or hard-to-verify fields are almost always absent, and geolocation and weather fields tend to be missing together, which points to a missing-not-at-random (MNAR) mechanism. Listwise deletion is therefore not viable. Only 57 records (3.5\%) are complete across all retained columns. Keeping only the columns below 20\% missingness leaves 853 complete records (52.8\%), and a 40\% threshold leaves only 403 (24.9\%).

The free-text categorical fields carried high cardinality, with 918 unique values across 11 fields, along with typos, casing drift, and verbose descriptions. We consolidated them into 156 canonical categories, an 83\% reduction. We normalised spelling and casing, reduced detailed place names to the home district, and collapsed verbose reason and method descriptions, while keeping small but distinct groups such as Chakma, Marma, and Santal as separate religious categories. A large language model (GPT-4) proposed the candidate mappings, and the authors reviewed and validated every one, overriding the model in three documented cases. The model was used only at design time, and the validated mappings ship as deterministic dictionaries, so the runtime pipeline is fully reproducible.

Before imputation, a deterministic recovery stage filled values that external sources make verifiable rather than estimated. Place names were geocoded to latitude and longitude, which cut their missingness from about 53\% to 6.5\%. A Unix timestamp was derived from the reported date and time, dropping from 52.4\% to 5\%. Weather was retrieved from the Meteostat API keyed on location and timestamp, which reduced temperature missingness from 53\% to about 30\%. A few fields such as humidity could not be recovered.

The 29 features were then encoded into two numeric forms. For the distance-based imputers KNN and MICE, the three low-cardinality nominal fields (gender, workplace, religion) were one-hot encoded into one binary column per category. This expands the three fields into eleven columns and gives a 37-column matrix ($29-3+11$). The high-cardinality fields were frequency encoded to avoid the dimensional blow-up of one-hot on many-valued features~\cite{pargent2022highcardinality}. For MissForest, which handles categoricals natively, all 29 features were label encoded, keeping one column per feature.

This recovery stage is shared by every downstream dataset, so the study reduces to a single controlled decision about the columns that remain severely incomplete. The impute arm keeps those columns and fills them statistically. The drop arm discards columns above a missingness threshold, either 20\% or 40\%, and then keeps only complete rows. Table~\ref{tbl:design} lists the five resulting datasets. MICE keeps 1{,}613 rows because four records lacked usable predictors. Any later difference between the arms comes from the imputation choice and not from preprocessing.

\subsection{Architecture}
This subsection details the modelling pipeline, and Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives the overview.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth,keepaspectratio]{intro.png}
  \caption{High-level study pipeline, from data collection and preprocessing through encoding, the five datasets, clustering, and composite scoring.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

The pipeline takes the cleaned and encoded data, forms the five datasets, clusters each one with K-Means and Agglomerative, and ranks the ten pipelines by the composite score. We describe the imputation, clustering, and scoring steps below.

\subsubsection{Imputation}
We evaluated three imputers from different paradigms, since no single imputer dominates across data types and missingness patterns~\cite{jager2021benchmark}. Each imputer used every available feature as a predictor and produced one imputed dataset. MissForest~\cite{stekhoven2012missforest} ran with 100 trees on the 29 label-encoded columns and iterated to the Stekhoven stopping criterion. KNN imputation~\cite{troyanskaya2001knn} used $k=5$ with Euclidean distance on the 37 one-hot columns. MICE~\cite{vanbuuren2011mice} used a per-variable Bayesian ridge estimator in a single pass over the same columns. All stochastic parts used a fixed seed, and residual missingness stayed below 0.25\%.

\subsubsection{Clustering}
Each dataset was standardised and then reduced with PCA before clustering. We kept 99\% of the variance for K-Means and ten components for Agglomerative. This matters because the matrix mixes one-hot, frequency, and label encodings, and standardisation with PCA places them on comparable scales for Euclidean distance. K-Means~\cite{macqueen1967kmeans} was tuned by grid search over the number of clusters from 2 to 20, the initialisation, the number of initialisations, and the iteration limit. Agglomerative clustering with Ward-style linkage~\cite{ward1963hierarchical} was tuned over linkage and distance metric with the number of clusters from 2 to 10. These two cover the centroid-based and hierarchical families, and using different paradigms guards against mistaking an artefact of one method for real structure.

\subsubsection{Score}
With no ground-truth labels, we ranked pipelines by an internal composite. It combines five standard validity indices, silhouette, Calinski--Harabasz, Dunn, and the inverted Davies--Bouldin and Xie--Beni, with a study-specific balance penalty. The balance term rewards even cluster sizes and penalises over-segmentation, because the geometric indices alone reward near-degenerate partitions such as one huge cluster with a few orphan points. For a partition into $K\geq2$ clusters, where $p_k$ is the share of records in cluster $k$, it is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balance}
\text{balance} = \left(1 - \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl(p_k - \tfrac{1}{K}\Bigr)^{2}}\,\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}},
\end{equation}
The first factor falls as the cluster sizes grow uneven, and the $1/\sqrt{K}$ factor falls as the number of clusters grows. Balance is therefore highest for even, moderately segmented partitions.

The composite works on ranks rather than raw values, since the six metrics live on very different scales. On each metric the ten pipelines are ranked, and the best earns 10 points, the next 9, down to 1 for the worst. If $r_m$ is the zero-indexed rank on metric $m$, so the best pipeline has $r_m=0$, and $n=10$, the score is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:composite}
\text{score} \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{6} \omega_m \,(n - r_m),
\end{equation}
where $\omega_m$ is the weight on metric $m$ and the six weights sum to one. Any fixed weighting is a judgement call, so we treat the weights as free and test robustness to them in Section~\ref{sec:analysis-results}. Balance still deserves emphasis, since it is the one term that catches the degenerate partitions above, and silhouette is the field-standard separation index~\cite{chicco2025silhouette}. Our conclusions rely on neither choice.

\subsection{Algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} summarises the whole procedure, from the raw records to a weight-robust ranking of the pipelines.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Imputation and clustering pipeline}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Consolidate the free-text categories with design-time dictionaries
\State Recover location, time, and weather from external sources
\State Drop the five fields above 70\% missingness, keeping 29 features
\State Encode to a 37-column matrix (one-hot and frequency) and a 29-column matrix (label)
\State Form three imputed datasets with MissForest, KNN, and MICE
\State Form two baseline datasets by dropping high-missingness columns, then incomplete rows
\For{each of the five datasets}
    \State Standardise the features and reduce with PCA
    \State Cluster with K-Means and with Agglomerative, both tuned
    \State Compute the five validity indices and the balance term
\EndFor
\State Rank the ten pipelines by the composite score over 100{,}000 random weightings
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We implemented the whole pipeline in Python with scikit-learn. Every stochastic step, including the MissForest forests, the K-Means initialisation, and the PCA solver, used a fixed random seed, so the reported results are reproducible from the released code.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis-results}

The ten pipelines comprise six imputed cells and four no-imputation baselines. Each imputer is paired with both clustering algorithms, and each drop threshold is paired with the same two algorithms. Any single weighting of the six validity indices is a judgement call, so we test the ranking's robustness to the weights directly. We rank the ten pipelines under $100{,}000$ weight vectors drawn uniformly over the six-metric simplex. Table~\ref{tbl:rankdist} lists each pipeline with its balance and silhouette values, its mean rank, and how often it lands in the top four. Balance and silhouette are raw metric values and use no weighting. Mean rank is the average position out of ten, where one is best. The full six-metric values are in the released repository.

The result is decisive and weight-independent. The four strong imputed pipelines occupy the top four ranks in $99.9\%$ of all weightings. These are MissForest with either algorithm and MICE and KNN with K-Means. The two poorly-combined imputed pipelines and all four baselines are essentially never in the top four. The rare exceptions, about $0.06\%$, put almost all weight on the Dunn index, which is known to be unstable. Imputation therefore improves cluster validity no matter how the metrics are weighted. Even on silhouette alone, a standard separation index, the strong imputed pipelines score $0.755$ to $0.792$, above every baseline at $0.157$ or less. The single top rank alternates between MICE/KM at $61\%$ of weightings and MF/Agg at $39\%$. We therefore report a robust imputed-versus-baseline separation rather than a single best pipeline.

The weight-independent separation conceals a strong interaction between imputer and clustering algorithm. Under K-Means all three imputers rank among the strongest (mean ranks $1.5$--$3.5$). Under Agglomerative clustering, only MissForest survives (mean rank $1.7$). MICE and KNN collapse to \emph{negative} silhouettes ($-0.078$ and $-0.020$) and fall below the best no-imputation baseline, \texttt{None (40\%)} on Agglomerative. These two failures share a likely cause. Both rely on the one-hot representation, whose high-dimensional geometry Agglomerative linkage handles poorly. MissForest sidesteps this with a compact label encoding, and K-Means, which retained more variance in its projection, is less affected. Imputation therefore does not help unconditionally. Two of the six imputed pipelines are beaten by simply dropping data. The defensible claim is not ``impute and win'' but that the imputer and the clustering algorithm must be chosen together.

Imputation also stabilises the recovered structure. All four strong imputed pipelines produced clean $k{=}2$ solutions with the largest cluster holding $53$ to $73\%$ of the records. The no-imputation baselines instead either over-segment (up to $k{=}10$) or collapse into a mega-cluster with orphans. For example, \texttt{None (40\%)}/Agg partitions into $402$ and $1$ records. The two weaker imputed pipelines exhibit the same collapse. Both failure modes are exactly what the balance term demotes.

The strong pipelines converge on the same primary structure. Each leader splits the corpus into two clean, well-balanced clusters, and both boundaries follow the dataset's two-cohort history. That history is the 2020 subset against the manually collected 2017 to 2025 cases. MF/Agg splits into $759$ and $858$ records, and its smaller cluster holds only year-2020 cases. MICE/KM splits into $882$ and $731$ records with mean report years $2020.0$ and $2022.4$. It follows the same boundary less sharply. It gathers nearly every 2020 case, $761$ of $762$, into one cluster, along with about $120$ adjacent-year cases from 2019 and 2021. Figure~\ref{fig:clusters} shows this split, both as a PCA projection of the two clusters and as their report-year distribution.

No temporal or source field was given to the algorithms, so this split is emergent. It shows that imputation preserved the cohort-level structure in the data. This is the paper's central result. Principled imputation makes severely incomplete news data usable for unsupervised analysis, and the gain over dropping data holds no matter how the metrics are weighted. The source-cohort confound affects what the clusters mean, not whether imputation helps. We therefore claim structure recovery, not epidemiological discovery. Finer contrasts such as the workplace profile differ between pipelines, so we do not over-read them. A cohort-aligned demographic contrast would need homogeneously sourced data before it could guide prevention. With a single run per pipeline, we also do not claim the leaders are statistically distinguishable.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}\label{sec:limitation}
Our pipeline turns severely incomplete newspaper-reported suicide data into a usable basis for unsupervised analysis. This is the obstacle that keeps such data out of standard machine-learning workflows. We assembled a dataset for a regime that prior work had not treated as a modelling problem. We then benchmarked three imputers against no-imputation baselines under a balance-aware score, and we tested that score across $100{,}000$ weightings. Earlier work either clustered largely complete records or benchmarked imputation on artificially removed values, so none of it faced organically incomplete news data. Our results close that gap. Imputation improves cluster validity in over $99.9\%$ of weightings, and the strong pipelines recover the same cohort-aligned structure. The contribution is a clear demonstration that weight-independent structure recovery is achievable on this data. Careful imputation is therefore a feasible foundation for suicide surveillance under SDG-3.

The work has clear limits. The missing values are organically absent, so there is no ground truth for imputation accuracy. We therefore claim usable structure rather than accurate reconstruction of individual cells. The likely MNAR mechanism keeps the cluster-level claims exploratory. The recovered split also tracks the source cohort, so the cluster contrasts are confounded with collection source and should not be read as epidemiology. Each pipeline was run once without resampling confidence intervals, and the corpus only covers cases that reached the news.

Future work broadens the method grid to more imputers and clustering families, adds uncertainty quantification, and runs a mask-and-recover test to check reconstruction directly. Applying the same pipeline to other low-resource, news-derived datasets would show whether these findings generalise beyond Bangladesh.
